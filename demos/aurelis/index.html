I — Le Cadran

Forty-two hours on the rose engine.

Every wave on this dial was cut by hand on a machine built in 1911. The tool does not pause for a mistake. One slip and the plate goes back to the furnace, and the forty-two hours start again.

Eleven coats of midnight.

The blue is laid down in eleven lacquer coats, each polished back before the next is poured. The depth you are looking at is four tenths of a millimetre.

II — Le Calibre

Two hundred and fourteen components.

Forty-one of them are smaller than a grain of rice. Keep scrolling and the case will let go of every one.

in flight

Assembled twice.

Once to prove that it runs. Then taken apart again, finished by hand on the surfaces no owner will ever see, and assembled a second time — the only one that counts.

Ninety-six hours of reserve.

Two barrels in series. Set it down on a Friday evening and it is still keeping time when you pick it up on Tuesday.

III — La Complication

The moon, wrong by one day in 122 years.

A 135-tooth wheel turns the disc once every fifty-nine days. Set it tonight and your grandchildren will still find it true.

Aperture at six o'clock. Two moons, hand-painted, in a sky of pierced stars.

The atelier

Five watchmakers. One bench each. No production line.

Maison Aurelis has occupied the same three rooms above Rue du Rhône since 1908. Everything except the sapphire is made within four hundred metres of that door.

A watchmaker takes a calibre from bare plate to running movement alone. Their initials are struck on the underside of the barrel bridge, where only the next watchmaker to open it will ever read them.

Private viewing

A Nocturne is not sold across a counter.

Viewings are by appointment, one at a time, in the room where the watch was made. Allow two hours. There is coffee, and there is no obligation.
